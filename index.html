Case Analysis: Diabetic Readmission

Who comes back within 30 days?

A predictive model for 30-day hospital readmission risk in diabetic patients, built to show not just whether a patient will return, but which patients a hospital should watch most closely.

67,112 patients 52 features 2 models compared 5-fold cross-validated
Clean

Getting the data honest

The raw dataset covers 101,766 hospital encounters for diabetic patients across 130 US hospitals (1999–2008). Before any modeling, the data needed real cleanup: weight and payer code columns were dropped (96% and 40% missing, respectively), and rows missing race or diagnosis codes were removed rather than imputed.

The most important fix wasn't visible in a missing-values table at all: the raw data contains repeat encounters for the same patient. Left as-is, a patient could appear in both the training and test set, silently inflating the model's apparent accuracy. Deduplicating to one encounter per patient removed that leakage, along with dropping encounters that ended in death or hospice, where "readmission" isn't a meaningful outcome.

101,766 encounters → 67,112 unique patients
Engineer

Turning raw fields into signal

  • Age groups: the raw 10-year brackets collapsed into Young / Middle-aged / Senior
  • Medication load: total medications prescribed, and how many were changed (dose up or down) during the stay
  • Prior visit history: total outpatient + emergency + inpatient visits before this encounter, plus a flag for any prior inpatient stay
  • Target: a binary flag for readmission within 30 days, present in just 9% of patients

What the data says before any model touches it

Two signals stood out immediately. Senior patients read a noticeably higher readmission rate than younger groups. And prior inpatient history mattered more than age, for example patients who'd been admitted before came back at nearly double the rate of those who hadn't.

Model

Two models, one honest comparison

Logistic Regression and Random Forest were trained side by side: a linear model against an ensemble that can capture feature interactions. Both used class_weight='balanced', since a model that just always predicted "not readmitted" would already look 91% accurate without learning anything.

ROC-AUC is the metric that matters here, not accuracy while the cross-validation confirms both scores hold steady across five different splits of the data, rather than being a fluke of one lucky split. The two models land close together, which suggests the relationship between these features and readmission risk is mostly linear rather than driven by complex interactions.

From a probability to a plan

A raw probability score isn't useful to a care team on its own. Ranking patients by predicted risk and splitting them into tiers turns the model into something a hospital could actually act on, prioritizing follow-up calls or discharge planning for the patients who need it most.

Conclude

What this means

Readmission risk here is driven far more by care history than by any single lab value, prior inpatient stays, total prior visits, and discharge disposition consistently outweigh demographic factors. That's a more actionable finding than a bare accuracy number: it points toward monitoring care continuity, not just individual test results.

Next steps worth exploring: gradient boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM) to test whether non-linear methods can close the gap further, and incorporating the ICD-9 diagnosis codes excluded here, which likely carry additional signal.
